## Pedagogical design (key decisions)

This lecture introduces dummy variables as independent variables while integrating a critical discussion about interpreting coefficients on immutable characteristics as causal effects. The critical discussion happens **during the lecture itself** (no separate seminar activity; the Week 11 seminar is fully dedicated to PS2 Workshop 4).

### Lecture arc

1. **Technical intro** (slides 1-7): What dummies are, coding, reference categories, interpretation of B0 and B1. Kept brisk because students already understand regression mechanics from Weeks 8-10.

2. **Gender-ideology regression** (slides 8-13): Regress left-right ideology (e01, 0-10 scale) on a `woman` dummy using BES 2024 data with survey weights. Students run the do-file and interpret the output. Then: "Can we call this causal?"

3. **Counterfactual criterion** (slides 14-21): This is the new conceptual contribution. Students already know about confounders (Week 9) and reverse causality (Week 9). This lecture adds a third lens: even absent confounders, causal language requires a meaningful counterfactual. For immutable characteristics like gender, the counterfactual ("what if this person were not a woman?") is ill-defined.

   - The **Zuberi/Bonilla-Silva quote** is the nudging mechanism: *"Empirical results may be a way to understand what is happening; however, these same data tell us very little about why it happens."* It is presented alongside the discussion question, not after a failed causal interpretation.
   - Students brainstorm social processes (socialisation, structural inequality) that produce the gender gap, reinforcing that the coefficient conflates many factors.
   - A corrective slide clarifies: dummies are useful tools; the coefficient describes real group differences; the problem is only in causal interpretation.

4. **Pearson case and closing** (slides 22-27): Karl Pearson (founder of modern statistics) published "The Problem of Alien Immigration" (1928) in the Annals of Eugenics, using correlations of physical measurements of Jewish immigrant children to argue that observed differences were innate racial traits, not environmental. His concluding paragraph is quoted on a slide. The policy relevance: this was explicitly framed as an argument for immigration restriction, lending statistical authority to prejudiced conclusions. The lecture closes with a course recap and the message: "Data literacy makes good citizens."

### Key design principles

- Technical and critical content are integrated, not sequential
- The gender example is the vehicle, not the destination
- The Pearson case amplifies stakes without dominating
- The lecture ends on an empowering note, not cautionary
- Scope is limited to dummies as IVs (dummies as DVs deferred to Stats 2)
- BES data used throughout, consistent with rest of course

### The "how much to nudge" decision

The quote from Zuberi/Bonilla-Silva serves as the nudge. It is presented alongside the discussion question rather than after students attempt a causal interpretation. Rationale: students have been taught all term that correlation does not imply causation, so they are unlikely to naively assert causality. The pedagogical challenge is getting them to articulate *why* the causal interpretation fails in a new way (counterfactual, not just confounders).
