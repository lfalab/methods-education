Problem Set 2: Bivariate Analysis

4SSPP111: Statistics for Political Science I – 2025-2026 Semester 2

Overview

Problem Set 2 (PS2) is worth 25% of your final module grade. You will conduct a single bivariate analysis using one of the datasets provided in the module’s Shared Project Folder. The assignment asks you to formulate a research question, describe your variables, produce an appropriate visualisation, carry out a hypothesis test, and reflect on what the results can and cannot tell us about causality.

This is not a full research project. You are not expected to review literature, fit multiple models, or make policy recommendations. The goal is to demonstrate that you can apply the tools covered in this module — from formulating a hypothesis to interpreting a statistical test — in a clear, well-structured, and reproducible way.

  • Released: Reading Week (Week 6)
  • Final submission deadline: 8 April 2026
  • Submission via: KEATS
  • Word limit: 1,500 words (excluding tables, figures, and references)

Data

You must use one of the datasets available in the module’s data-raw folder on the Shared Project Folder:

  • British Election Study (BES)
  • Quality of Government (QoG)
  • Brexit Regional dataset
  • National Student Survey (NSS)

You may not use external datasets. You should consult the relevant codebook when selecting and describing your variables.

Note on the British Election Study (BES) dataset

The British Election Study is a survey dataset that includes sampling weights and a complex survey design. In applied research, these features are often incorporated into statistical analysis in order to obtain estimates that reflect the survey design. If you choose to analyse the BES for this assignment, you may proceed in one of two ways:

  1. Conduct your analysis using the standard Stata commands introduced in class (without setting the survey design or applying weights).
  2. Incorporate the survey design and weights, using the appropriate Stata commands demonstrated in class.

Either approach is acceptable for this assignment and will not affect your grade. If you do not incorporate weights, you should briefly acknowledge in your Discussion and Limitations section that the BES is a weighted survey dataset and that ignoring weights may affect the precision of estimates.

In both cases, the assessment focuses on the clarity of your research question, the appropriateness of your chosen statistical test, the correctness of your interpretation, and the quality of your reasoning. You are not assessed on whether you use weighted or unweighted estimation.

Structure

Your report should follow the five sections below:

1. Research Question and Hypothesis

Formulate one clear, correlational research question involving exactly two variables from the same dataset. Your research question should be empirical, specific, and answerable with the data you have selected. It should not assume causality.

State a hypothesis that specifies the expected relationship between the two variables. The hypothesis should be directional. For example, if the research question is about gender and vote choice, the hypothesis should specify whether men or women are more likely to support a given party (“Men are more likely to vote for Reform UK” instead of “Men and women have different levels of support for Reform UK.”)

2. Data and Variables

For each of your two variables, you should describe what the variable measures, with reference to the codebook, and classify the variable as nominal or numeric. You are allowed to generate new variables using data transformation commands in Stata; if you do so, be sure to specify what variables in the original dataset were transformed to generate your new variable.

3. Descriptive Analysis

Produce appropriate univariate descriptive statistics for each variable. For nominal variables, you should produce a frequency table or bar chart showing the distribution of categories. For numeric variables, you should produce summary statistics (e.g. mean, median, standard deviation) and/or a histogram. Use the descriptive statistics to briefly characterise each variable before turning to the bivariate analysis.

4. Bivariate Analysis and Hypothesis Test

Produce one appropriate bivariate visualisation (e.g. a bivariate frequency chart, a bar chart of means, or a scatterplot), chosen according to the types of variables in your analysis. Then conduct exactly one bivariate hypothesis test (difference in proportions test, difference in means test, bivariate regression analysis). The appropriate chart and test should be selected according to the following decision rule:

Variable 1 Variable 2 Chart Test
Nominal Nominal Bivariate frequency chart Difference in proportions test
Nominal Numeric Bar chart of means Difference in means test
Numeric Numeric Scatterplot and regression line Bivariate regression analysis

Your report should state your hypothesis, the null hypothesis, and report the relevant test outputs (e.g. estimated difference, confidence interval, p-value, regression coefficient). Interpret the result in plain language: Explicitly state whether the null hypothesis is rejected at the 95% confidence level.

It is not a problem if the result is that we fail to reject the null hypothesis. The goal of research is not to prove that your hypothesis is correct; it’s to conduct the hypothesis test appropriately.

5. Discussion and Limitations

Interpret your findings in relation to your research question and hypothesis. State clearly whether you reject or fail to reject the null hypothesis, and explain what this result implies in substantive terms. The goal is not to confirm your expectations, but to interpret the statistical evidence accurately.

Next, reflect on the limits of causal inference in this design. Even if you observe a statistically significant association, this does not establish that one variable causes the other. Discuss the possible direction(s) of causality between the variables.

You should also discuss potential confounding variables. When conducting bivariate analysis, the omission of confounding variables can either underestimate or overestimate the relationship between two variables. Your report should consider potential confounding variables in your analysis. You are not expected to conduct an additional analysis to control for potential confounders. Your theoretical reasoning about them is sufficient.

Conclude the section with a brief paragraph that restates your research question and summarises what can and cannot be concluded from your analysis. Focus on what the statistical evidence suggests about the association between the two variables.

Submission Requirements

You must submit two files via KEATS:

  1. A written report in Word (.docx) or PDF format, structured as described above.
  2. A single Stata do-file (.do) that reproduces all tables and figures in your report.

The do-file must run from top to bottom without errors; include a header comment block with your name, the date, and a brief description of the file; and include comments throughout that explain what each section of code does. Do not include Stata code in the body of your written report.

Collaboration Policy

You may work individually or in a pair (two students). If you choose to collaborate, you may work together on variable selection, hypothesis formulation, data preparation, visualisation, and statistical testing. Your Stata do-files may be identical, and you may use the same variables and the same statistical test.

However, each student must submit an individual written report. Interpretation, reasoning, discussion of causality, and conclusions must be your own work. If you collaborate with someone, you must state in the title page of your submission who you collaborated with (candidate number). Submitting jointly written reports will be treated as an academic integrity violation.
