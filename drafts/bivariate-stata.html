    Bivariate analysis: What is it?

    • Univariate analysis describes one variable at a time (e.g., distribution of vote choice).
    • Bivariate analysis describes relationships between two variables (e.g., gender and vote choice).
    • The appropriate tool depends on the types of variables being analysed.

    Case 1: Nominal + Nominal

    • Example: Gender and vote choice.

    • Tools:

      • Cross-tabulation (with row or column percentages)
      • Bivariate bar chart

    Case 1: Cross-tabulation in Stata

    • The command tabulate b02 y09, column produces a cross-tabulation of vote choice by gender, with column percentages.
    • Column percentages allow us to compare the distribution of vote choice across gender groups.
    • The nofreq option removes the raw counts and displays only percentages.

    Case 1: Bivariate bar chart in Stata

    • The command catplot y09 b02, asyvars produces a grouped bar chart.
    • Each bar represents a category of vote choice, and bars are grouped by gender.
    • This visualises the same pattern shown in the cross-tabulation.

    Case 2: Nominal + Numeric

    • Example: Favourite newspaper and political ideology (left-right scale).

    • Tools:

      • Compare means by category
      • Bar chart of means

    Case 2: Comparing means in Stata

    • The command sort k03 sorts the dataset by newspaper readership.
    • The command by k03: summarize e01 calculates the mean ideology for each newspaper readership group.
    • This allows us to compare the average ideology across different newspapers.

    Case 2: Bar chart of means in Stata

    • The command graph hbar e01, over(k03) produces a bar chart showing the mean ideology for each newspaper group.
    • The height of each bar represents the average left-right self-placement for readers of that newspaper.
    • This visualises the pattern of association between newspaper readership and ideology.

    Case 3: Numeric + Numeric

    • Example: Import shock and share of Leave votes in the Brexit referendum.

    • Tools:

      • Scatterplot
      • Regression line

    Your turn: Explore the Brexit dataset

    • Download Brexit_Regional.dta from the data-raw folder in the Shared Project Folder.
    • Download bivariate-brexit.do from the do folder.
    • This do-file shows how to create scatterplots and regression lines for numeric variables.

    Scatterplots in Stata

    • The command twoway (scatter leave_share import_shock) produces a scatterplot.
    • Each point represents a UK region, plotted by its import shock (x-axis) and share of Leave votes (y-axis).
    • The scatterplot reveals the pattern of association between the two variables.

    Adding a regression line in Stata

    • The command twoway (scatter leave_share import_shock) (lfit leave_share import_shock) adds a regression line to the scatterplot.
    • The regression line summarises the overall relationship between the two variables.
    • An upward slope indicates a positive association; a downward slope indicates a negative association.

    Interpreting the scatterplot and regression line

    • The scatterplot shows individual data points; the regression line summarises the general pattern.
    • If the points are closely clustered around the line, the relationship is strong.
    • If the points are widely scattered, the relationship is weak.

    Your turn: Find additional relationships to visualise

    • tabulate with the column option gives us cross-tabulations for nominal variables.
    • catplot with the asyvars option gives us bivariate bar charts.
    • graph hbar with the over() option gives us bar charts of means.
    • twoway (scatter) and (lfit) give us scatterplots and regression lines.
    • Explore the BES and Brexit datasets to look for additional relationships.

    Recap

    • Cross-tabulations and grouped bar charts describe associations between nominal variables.
    • Bar charts of means summarise differences in a numeric variable across categories.
    • Scatterplots and regression lines summarise relationships between two numeric variables.
    • The appropriate tool depends on the types of variables being analysed.

    Why this matters:

    • Describing relationships between variables is the foundation for asking why those relationships exist.
    • Choosing the wrong tool for the wrong variable types produces meaningless or misleading output.
    • These skills are essential for interpreting bivariate analyses in research papers, reports, and public debate.
