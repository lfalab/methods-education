Describing Correlations: Bivariate Analysis

Statistics for Political Science — Week 5

Author

Leonardo Falabella

Introduction

In this activity, you will practise choosing the right analytical tool for different types of variable pairs, producing the relevant output in Stata, and interpreting what you find. Work through each exercise in order, discussing your choices and interpretations with your group before moving on.

For each exercise, your first task is always the same: identify the type of each variable, decide which case applies, and choose the appropriate tool. Only then should you proceed to Stata.

The dataset for all exercises is the Quality of Government (QoG) Basic Cross-Section dataset (qog_bas_cs_jan26.dta). Refer to the QoG codebook if you need more detail about any variable.

Exercise 1

Variables: bmr_dem and ht_colonial

  • bmr_dem — Dichotomous democracy measure (Boix, Miller, and Rosato). This is a binary variable: 0 = not a democracy, 1 = democracy. Countries are coded as democratic if political leaders are chosen through free and fair elections and a minimum suffrage threshold is met.
  • ht_colonial — Colonial origin. A categorical variable recording the country’s former Western overseas colonial ruler (e.g. British, French, Spanish, Portuguese, Belgian). Countries that were never colonised by a Western overseas power are coded 0.

Research question: Do countries with different colonial histories differ in their likelihood of being democratic today?

Tasks:

  1. Identify the type of each variable. What analytical case does this pair belong to? What tool should you use?
  2. Produce the appropriate table in Stata. Try both row and column percentages. Which orientation is more informative for answering the research question? Why?
  3. Produce the appropriate chart.
  4. Looking at your output: are some colonial origins more strongly associated with democratic status than others? Which patterns stand out?

Hint: Think about what row percentages and column percentages each tell you. Row percentages show the breakdown of one variable within each category of the other — switching between them can reveal different aspects of the relationship.

Exercise 2

Variables: gol_est and ht_region

  • gol_est — Electoral system type (Bormann and Golder). A categorical variable with three values: 1 = Majoritarian, 2 = Proportional, 3 = Mixed.
  • ht_region — Region of the world. A categorical variable classifying countries into ten politico-geographic regions, including Eastern Europe and post-Soviet Union, Latin America, North Africa and the Middle East, Sub-Saharan Africa, Western Europe and North America, East Asia, South-East Asia, South Asia, the Pacific, and the Caribbean.

Research question: Do countries in different world regions tend to use different electoral systems?

Tasks:

  1. Identify the type of each variable. What analytical case does this pair belong to? What tool should you use?
  2. Produce the appropriate table and chart in Stata.
  3. Looking at your output: does electoral system type appear to be evenly distributed across regions, or does it cluster? Are there any regions where one system type clearly dominates?
  4. Why might the choice of electoral system vary across world regions? Think about historical legacies, colonial influences, and regional diffusion of institutional models.

Hint: A bivariate chart will make regional patterns easier to see than a table alone. Look for categories that are heavily concentrated in one region or absent from another.

Exercise 3

Variables: ht_colonial and ti_cpi

  • ht_colonial — Colonial origin. See Exercise 1 for the full description.
  • ti_cpi — Corruption Perceptions Index (Transparency International). A continuous variable ranging from 0 to 100, where 0 means highly corrupt and 100 means highly clean. It captures perceptions of public sector corruption as seen by business people, risk analysts, and the general public.

Research question: Do countries with different colonial histories differ in their average levels of perceived corruption today?

Tasks:

  1. Identify the type of each variable. What analytical case does this pair belong to? What tool should you use?
  2. Produce the appropriate summary table and chart in Stata.
  3. Looking at your output: which colonial-origin groups have the highest and lowest average corruption scores? Are the differences large or small?
  4. What might explain any patterns you observe? Consider what else differs across countries with different colonial histories.

Hint: The bar chart of means lets you compare average corruption scores across colonial-origin categories at a glance. Look for categories with notably higher or lower means, and consider whether any categories have few countries — this affects how reliable the average is.

Exercise 4

Variables: gol_est and ideavt_legvt

  • gol_est — Electoral system type. See Exercise 2 for the full description.
  • ideavt_legvt — Parliamentary election voter turnout (Institute for Democracy and Electoral Assistance). A continuous variable measuring the percentage of the voting-age population that cast a vote in the most recent parliamentary election, ranging from 0 to 100.

Research question: Do countries with different electoral systems differ in their average levels of voter turnout?

Tasks:

  1. Identify the type of each variable. What analytical case does this pair belong to? What tool should you use?
  2. Produce the appropriate summary table and chart in Stata.
  3. Looking at your output: which electoral system is associated with the highest average turnout? Which with the lowest? Is the difference substantively meaningful?
  4. Why might electoral system type be associated with turnout? Think about how different systems create incentives for parties to mobilise voters.

Hint: Compare the means across the three electoral system categories. A difference of a few percentage points in turnout can be politically significant at the national level, even if it looks modest in a chart.

Exercise 5

Variables: wdi_gini and wdi_wip

  • wdi_gini — Gini index (World Bank). A continuous variable measuring income inequality within a country. It ranges from 0 (perfect equality, where everyone has the same income) to 100 (perfect inequality, where one person has all the income). Higher values mean more inequality.
  • wdi_wip — Proportion of seats held by women in national parliaments (World Bank). A continuous variable measured as a percentage, ranging from 0 to 100.

Research question: Do more unequal countries tend to have lower levels of women’s representation in parliament?

Tasks:

  1. Identify the type of each variable. What analytical case does this pair belong to? What tool should you use?
  2. Produce the appropriate chart in Stata, including a regression line.
  3. Looking at your output: what does the slope of the regression line tell you about the direction of the relationship? Is the relationship strong (points clustered close to the line) or weak (points widely scattered)?
  4. Is the pattern what you expected? Can you think of countries or regions that might pull the pattern in one direction or another?

Hint: Pay attention to both the direction of the regression line (is it upward or downward sloping?) and how tightly the points cluster around it. A flat or nearly flat line suggests little association, even if the slope is not exactly zero.

Exercise 6

Variables: wdi_gini and ti_cpi

  • wdi_gini — Gini index. See Exercise 5 for the full description.
  • ti_cpi — Corruption Perceptions Index. See Exercise 3 for the full description.

Research question: Do more unequal countries tend to have higher levels of perceived corruption?

Tasks:

  1. Identify the type of each variable. What analytical case does this pair belong to? What tool should you use?
  2. Produce the appropriate chart in Stata, including a regression line.
  3. Looking at your output: what is the direction of the association? Does it match the theoretical expectation that high inequality and high corruption tend to go together?
  4. Compare what you see here with Exercise 5. Both exercises use wdi_gini on the horizontal axis but pair it with a different variable. What does this tell you about the relationship between inequality, corruption, and gender representation?

Hint: Note which end of the ti_cpi scale means more corruption (lower scores) and which means less (higher scores). This matters for interpreting the direction of the slope correctly.

Exercise 7

Variables: wdi_internet and rsf_pfi

  • wdi_internet — Individuals using the internet (World Bank). A continuous variable measuring the percentage of the population with internet access, ranging from 0 to 100.
  • rsf_pfi — Press Freedom Index (Reporters Without Borders). A continuous variable measuring the degree of freedom available to journalists and media organisations in each country. Higher scores indicate more press freedom.

Research question: Do countries with higher internet usage tend to have greater press freedom?

Tasks:

  1. Identify the type of each variable. What analytical case does this pair belong to? What tool should you use?
  2. Produce the appropriate chart in Stata, including a regression line.
  3. Looking at your output: is there a positive association, a negative association, or no clear pattern?
  4. Internet access and press freedom could be related in multiple ways — higher access could reflect or enable more open media environments, or both could be driven by a third factor such as economic development or regime type. What do you think the scatterplot is actually capturing?

Hint: Before interpreting the slope, make sure you have the direction of the rsf_pfi scale clearly in mind: a higher score means more freedom, not less. If the slope is positive, that means more internet usage is associated with more press freedom.

Reflection

After completing all seven exercises, discuss the following with your group:

  1. Which exercises required the most thought when identifying the variable types? What made them more difficult?
  2. In the exercises involving cross-tabulations, how did switching between row and column percentages change what you could see? When is each orientation more useful?
  3. Across the scatterplot exercises, how did you judge whether a relationship was strong or weak? What features of the plot did you use?
  4. In several exercises, the variables used are measures of the same underlying concept from different sources (e.g. corruption appears in Exercises 3 and 6). What are the implications of this for how we interpret the results?

Summary

In this activity, you have:

  • Identified variable types and matched them to the appropriate bivariate analytical tool.
  • Produced and interpreted cross-tabulations, bar charts of means, and scatterplots with regression lines.
  • Connected descriptive patterns in data to theoretical arguments in political science.

These skills form the foundation for the independent empirical analysis you will carry out in Problem Set 2.
