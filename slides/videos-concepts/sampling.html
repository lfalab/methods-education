Why Sampling Matters

You’ll learn to

  • Define a population and a sample.
  • Explain how we use samples to draw conclusions about populations.
  • Explain why how data is collected affects what conclusions we can make.

We want to know about populations

  • Once we have a research question and hypothesis, we want to collect data.
  • Ideally, we want data on every unit we are interested in.
  • The full set of units we want to draw conclusions about is called the population.

Populations depend on the question

  • The population is defined by who or what we are studying.

    • All voters in a general election.
    • All countries in the world.
    • All cities in the United Kingdom.

Populations do not have to be people — they are whatever units our question is about.

The problem: populations are often inaccessible

  • Collecting data on every unit in a population is often too expensive or too slow.
  • Sometimes it is simply impossible.
  • We need a way to learn about the population without studying all of it.

The solution: samples

  • A sample is a subset of units drawn from the population.
  • We collect data on the sample and use it to reason about the population.
  • This is exactly what opinion polls do before an election.

From sample to population: inference

  • Drawing conclusions about a population using a sample is called inference.
  • But we can only make valid inferences from certain types of samples.
  • This is why how data is collected matters a lot.

Not all samples are equal

  • Imagine a news outlet that polls only its own viewers.
  • If viewers skew towards one political side, the sample will over-represent those views.
  • The sample will not look like the population — and any inference will be misleading.

Non-probability samples

  • In a non-probability sample, not every unit in the population has a chance of being selected.
  • These samples tend to be unrepresentative.
  • We cannot use non-probability samples to make valid inferences about the population.

Probability samples

  • In a probability sample, every unit in the population has a known chance of being selected.
  • The most important type is the simple random sample.
  • In a random sample, every unit has an equal probability of being chosen.

Why random samples are powerful

  • Random samples produce samples that are representative of the population.
  • The distribution of characteristics in the sample will closely mirror the population.
  • This allows us to generalise from the sample to the population with confidence.

Recap

  • A population is the full set of units we want to draw conclusions about.
  • A sample is a subset of the population that we actually observe.
  • Inference means using a sample to draw conclusions about a population.
  • Random samples are representative and support valid inference; non-probability samples do not.

Why this matters:

  • Almost all political science data comes from samples, not full populations.
  • Understanding how a sample was collected determines what conclusions we can draw.
