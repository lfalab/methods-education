Exam Review Guide

This page is a topic-by-topic guide for revising for the 4SSPP111 final exam. Topics are grouped by the resources that prepare you for them: each block lists what you should be able to do, then the videos, slides, and activity that cover the whole block.

Practical exam information

  • Duration: 2 hours.
  • Total marks: 100, across 12 questions in three parts.
  • Calculator: Casio Fx83 or Fx85 only.
  • Notes allowed: one sheet of A4 paper with handwritten notes on both sides.
  • No Stata commands are tested. The exam assesses reasoning and interpretation, not Stata syntax.
  • Answer all questions.

How to use this guide

For each topic block:

  1. Watch the linked concept videos.
  2. Review the corresponding lecture slides.
  3. Redo the seminar activity from memory, then check your answers.

When you have worked through the blocks, attempt the Mock Exam (available on KEATS) under timed conditions, then compare your answers against the Mock Exam Answer Key (also on KEATS). The mock does not cover every possible exam topic, so do not treat it as an exhaustive list.

Part 1 — Descriptive analysis

Research questions, theories, and hypotheses

  • Distinguish empirical from normative questions.
  • Identify dependent and independent variables in a quantitative claim.
  • Recognise the role of theory and hypothesis in framing a quantitative claim.

Measuring variables

  • Evaluate how a chosen measurement strategy shapes the conclusions a piece of research can support.

Variable types

  • Classify a variable as nominal, ordinal, interval, or ratio.
  • Justify the classification by reference to its measurement properties (categories, ordering, equal intervals, true zero).

Distributions: histograms, shape, and central tendency

  • Read approximate counts from histogram bars and compare counts across ranges.
  • Identify whether a distribution is symmetric, left-skewed, or right-skewed.
  • Predict whether the mean is greater than, less than, or approximately equal to the median based on the shape of the distribution, and explain why.

Descriptive vs causal claims

  • Identify whether a claim is descriptive or causal.
  • Explain the wording or reasoning that makes it one or the other.

Part 2 — Sampling, estimation, and uncertainty

Population, sampling, and bias

  • Identify the population of interest in a research scenario.
  • Classify a sampling procedure as probability or non-probability, and justify the classification by reference to whether every member of the population has a known, non-zero chance of selection.
  • Assess whether a sampling procedure is likely to produce a biased estimate, predict the direction of the bias, and explain the mechanism that produces it.

Resources:

  • Concept video: Why Sampling Matters
  • Lecture: Week 7: Estimation, Uncertainty, and Statistical Inference
  • Activity: Sampling and Bias

Part 3 — Regression and causal inference

Bivariate regression: slope and intercept

  • State the predicted change in the dependent variable associated with a one-unit change in the independent variable, including units and direction.
  • State the predicted value of the dependent variable when the independent variable equals zero (the intercept).

Resources:

  • Concept video: Regression Analysis: Introduction

  • Lecture: Week 8: Regression Analysis
  • Activity: Bivariate Regression

Multivariate regression and predicted values

  • Read a multivariate regression table.
  • Compute a predicted value of the dependent variable given values for each independent variable, using the regression equation. Show your calculations.

Resources:

Statistical significance

  • Read significance stars in a regression table and identify which coefficients are statistically significant.
  • Explain what non-significance means in terms of the null hypothesis.
  • Relate the size of a coefficient to its standard error.

Confounding, reverse causality, and random assignment

  • Define a confounding variable, propose a plausible confounder for a given regression, and justify the proposal by explaining how the confounder relates to both the independent and dependent variables.
  • Assess whether a causal claim can plausibly be challenged on grounds of reverse causality, and explain the direction of the alternative causal pathway.
  • Explain how random assignment supports causal inference, and why observational regressions cannot, on their own, support the same kind of causal claim.

Dummy variables and counterfactual reasoning

  • Interpret the intercept and slope of a regression that includes a dummy variable as the independent variable.
  • Assess whether a counterfactual implied by a regression specification is plausible, and explain why the plausibility of the counterfactual matters for the interpretation of an estimate.

Final preparation

Once you have worked through the blocks above, attempt the Mock Exam (available on KEATS) under timed conditions: two hours, calculator, one A4 sheet of handwritten notes.
