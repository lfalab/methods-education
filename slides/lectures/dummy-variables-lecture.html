What is a dummy variable?

  • A variable coded 0 or 1.
  • Represents membership in a category.
  • The group coded 0 is the reference category.

Examples of dummy variables

Dummy variable Coded 1 if… Reference group (0)
Woman respondent is a woman men
Graduate respondent has a degree non-graduates
Dictatorship country is a dictatorship democracies

What can you do with a dummy?

  • Sum gives the count of observations coded 1.
  • Mean gives the proportion coded 1.
  • Useful way to include nominal variables in regression.

Dummy as independent variable

  • The model: Y = B0 + B1(dummy)
  • B0 = predicted Y for the reference group (coded 0).
  • B1 = predicted difference for the group coded 1.

Interpretation example

  • Suppose you regress income on a Graduate dummy.
  • B0 = average income for non-graduates.
  • B1 = how much more (or less) graduates earn, on average.

Today’s question

  • Are women more left-wing than men?
  • Data: British Election Study 2024.
  • Ideology: 0 (left) to 10 (right) self-placement scale.

Building the dummy in Stata

replace y09 = . if y09 < 0
replace e01 = . if e01 < 0
keep if y09 == 1 | y09 == 2

generate woman = (y09 == 2) if y09 != .
  • y09: gender variable (1 = male, 2 = female).
  • woman: our dummy (1 = woman, 0 = man).

Your Turn: Run the regression

  • In the do-file, find and run the svy: regress command.
  • What is the coefficient on woman?
  • How would you interpret it in plain language?

The result

svy: regress e01 woman

Survey: Linear regression            Number of obs = 2,652

--------------------------------------------------------------
         e01 | Coefficient  std. err.      t    P>|t|
-------------+------------------------------------------------
       woman |  -.3955786   .0853191    -4.64   0.000
       _cons |   4.981491   .0634301    78.54   0.000
--------------------------------------------------------------

Interpreting the output

  • Intercept (4.98): men’s predicted ideology score.
  • Woman (-0.40): women score 0.40 points lower (more left).
  • Statistically significant (p < 0.001).

Can we call this causal?

  • The coefficient is real and precisely estimated.
  • But does being a woman cause someone to be more left-wing?
  • This is a different question from “is there an association?”

What you already know

  • In Week 9, we discussed two threats to causal claims.
  • Confounding: a third variable drives both X and Y.
  • Reverse causality: Y might cause X instead.

A third consideration: the counterfactual

  • Causal claims require a meaningful “what if?”
  • “What if this person had not received the treatment?”
  • In an experiment, the counterfactual is clear.

The counterfactual problem

  • “What would this person’s ideology be if they were not a woman?”
  • This question does not have a clear answer.
  • Gender is not a treatment we can assign or withhold.

Discussion

“Empirical results may be a way to understand what is happening; however, these same data tell us very little about why it happens.”

– Tukufu Zuberi and Eduardo Bonilla-Silva, White Logic, White Methods

  • Does the gender coefficient tell us why men and women differ?

Your Turn: Why might they differ?

  • Brainstorm with your neighbour: what social processes could produce this gap?
  • If we controlled for those, would the coefficient survive?
  • What does this tell us about the coefficient’s meaning?

What the coefficient tells us

  • Women are, on average, more left-leaning than men.
  • The difference is precisely estimated and significant.
  • This is useful for description and prediction.

What the coefficient does not tell us

  • Not what would happen if gender were “changed.”
  • It conflates socialisation, experience, and structural factors.
  • Causal language requires assumptions we cannot test.

Dummy variables as dependent variables

  • What if the dummy is on the left-hand side of the equation?
  • Interpretation changes: coefficients become predicted probabilities.
  • This will be covered in Stats 2 next year.

When this goes wrong

  • Karl Pearson: one of the founders of modern statistics.
  • In 1928, he studied “alien Jewish children” in London schools.
  • He measured skull shapes, eyesight, and pigmentation.

“The Problem of Alien Immigration” (1928)

  • Pearson found group differences on physical measures.
  • He concluded these were innate racial traits.
  • “Not the product of the immediate environment.”

Pearson’s conclusion

“[Poor sight] is not the product of the immediate environment, nor likely to be substantially influenced by a change in that environment; it is rather a racial character only to be modified by selective action through many generations.”

– Karl Pearson and Margaret Moul, Annals of Eugenics, 1928

The error

  • Pearson read demographic correlations as biological causation.
  • He ignored poverty, discrimination, and language barriers.
  • He used statistical authority to support immigration restriction.

What you have learned this term

  • Describe distributions, correlations, and regressions.
  • Test hypotheses with confidence intervals and p-values.
  • Evaluate causal claims with confounders and counterfactuals.

Why this matters

  • Statistics is a tool for careful thinking about evidence.
  • Knowing what your tools cannot do is just as important.
  • Data literacy makes good citizens.
