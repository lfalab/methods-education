How PS1 Works (Core Rules)

Each week:

  • Videos introduce key concepts
  • Quizzes test understanding of those concepts
  • Lecture and seminar activities assume this preparation

PS1 is designed to support learning over time, not last-minute revision

Quizzes vs Videos (Important Distinction)

    • Unlimited attempts
    • Highest score counts

Quizzes vs Videos (Important Distinction)

    • Include attention-check questions
    • One attempt per question
    • Videos remain accessible throughout the term

Flexibility Built In

  • This is designed as insurance, not a target

Research Questions: Descriptive vs Causal

  • Empirical questions can be descriptive or causal.
  • Descriptive questions ask what the world looks like.
  • Causal questions ask what would happen if we intervened.

Descriptive questions

  • Descriptive questions can be about distributions, trends, or correlations.

    • What percentage of countries are democratic?
    • Is there a global trend towards democratic backsliding?
    • Is democratic backsliding more prevalent in countries with higher political polarisation?

Causal questions

  • Causal questions ask if some variables drive change in others.

    • Does exposure to divisive content on social media make people more tolerant of political violence?
    • Does declining trust in political elites weaken commitment to democratic norms?
    • Do compulsory voting laws increase political participation?

Complementarities between questions

  • Describing a distribution:

    • What percentage of the world’s wealth is held by the 50 richest people?

  • Describing a trend:

    • Is economic inequality increasing across the world?

Complementarities between questions

  • Describing a correlation:

    • Are autocracies more unequal than democracies?

  • Probing causation:

    • Do increases in income inequality make democratic backsliding more likely?

Theories

  • A theory is “a reasoned and precise speculation about the answer to a research question” (King, Keohane, and Verba 1994:3).
  • Based on our knowledge and reasoning, what’s a plausible answer to a research question?

Example: Health care systems and policy responses

  • Research question: How did countries’ health care systems affect their abilities to mitigate the impact of the Covid-19 pandemic?

  • Theory: Countries with public health care systems took advantage of their centralised bureaucracies to implement more effective policy responses.

Hypotheses

  • A hypothesis is an empirically testable implication of a theory.
  • Given your theory, what should we observe in the world?

Example: Health care systems and policy responses

  • Theory: Countries with public health care systems took advantage of their centralised bureaucracies to implement more effective policy responses.

  • Hypothesis: Countries with public health care systems had lower Covid death rates than countries with private health care systems.

Group Activity: Research Questions, Theories, and Hypotheses

Working in groups of about three. By the end of the activity, each group should have:

  • One clearly stated research question
  • A decision about whether the question is descriptive or causal
  • One theory that offers a a reasoned and precise speculation about the answer to your question
  • One hypothesis that follows logically from the theory

Group Activity: Research Questions, Theories, and Hypotheses

General guidance:

  • The research question should come first and guide everything else
  • The theory should explain why, not just what
  • The hypothesis should be empirically testable
  • Descriptive questions can still have theories and hypotheses

Why This Matters for the Module

  • This structure, question → theory → hypothesis, will reappear throughout the module and your future research projects.
  • These are foundational concepts where misunderstandings are very common.
