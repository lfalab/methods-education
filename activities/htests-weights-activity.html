Seminar Activity: Weights and Visual Inference

Statistics for Political Science — Week 7

Author

Leonardo Falabella

Two ideas for today

  • What are survey weights, and why do we use them?
  • How can we use plots with confidence intervals to visually approximate a hypothesis test?

What is a survey?

  • A survey samples people from a larger target population.
  • We want our estimates to represent that population — not just who happened to respond.
  • But some groups are harder to reach than others.

The problem: unequal representation

  • Young men, low-income households, and rural residents tend to respond less.
  • This means the raw sample is not a mirror image of the population.
  • Estimates from an unweighted sample can be systematically biased.

What weights do

  • Each respondent is assigned a weight that corrects for their under- or over-representation.
  • A weight above 1 means the respondent’s group is underrepresented in the sample.
  • A weight below 1 means their group is overrepresented.

Weights in the BES

  • The BES uses a demographic post-stratification weight (wt_demog).
  • It adjusts for age, sex, and region.
  • Weighted estimates better reflect the voting-eligible population.

From tables to plots

  • So far, inference has meant reading a table: find diff, find the 95% CI, check if it includes 0.
  • Plots with confidence intervals let us do the same thing visually.
  • This is how results are typically presented in published research.

The visual heuristic

  • Each group gets a point estimate (dot) and a 95% confidence interval (bar).
  • Ask: does one group’s dot lie outside the other group’s interval?

The rule of thumb

  • Point estimate clearly outside the other group’s CI → groups likely differ significantly.
  • Point estimate inside the other group’s CI → difference likely not significant.
  • This is a heuristic: it can be conservative in borderline cases.

Heuristic vs formal test

  • The visual check gives intuition — it is not the definitive answer.
  • The formal decision rule remains: does the CI for the difference include 0?
  • Use the plot to orient yourself; use the test output to confirm.

Today’s activity

  • Open hypothesis-bes.do and run it in two stages per test.
  • Use the plots to apply the visual heuristic.
  • Then verify against the formal inferential output.

Activity

This activity uses the British Election Study (BES) 2024. You will run two hypothesis tests — each in an unweighted and a survey-weighted version — and use the visual heuristic introduced above to approximate each conclusion before checking the formal output.

The companion do-file is hypothesis-bes.do. It contains all the commands you need.

Setup

  1. Access our Shared Project Folder.
  2. Click on do and download hypothesis-bes.do.
  3. Save it to your do folder and update the working directory.
  4. Read through the file from top to bottom before running anything.

Test 1 — Gender and Reform vote share

  • Research question: Are men more likely than women to have voted for Reform UK?
  • Alternative Hypothesis (H1): Men are more likely to vote Reform than women.
  • Null Hypothesis (H0): No difference between men and women in their likelihood of voting Reform.

Stage 1: Unweighted

Execute: Run the descriptive tabulation and the prtest command.

Interpret:

  • What share of men voted Reform? What share of women?
  • What is the estimated difference (diff)? Is it positive or negative?
  • Does the 95% confidence interval for diff include zero?
  • Do we reject or fail to reject H0?

Stage 2: Survey-weighted

Execute: Run the svyset declaration, svy: proportion, svy: regress, and the marginsplot commands.

Visual heuristic — inspect the plot:

  • Locate the point estimate for men and the confidence interval for women.
  • Does the men’s point estimate lie inside or outside the women’s confidence interval?
  • Based on this visual check alone: would you reject or fail to reject H0?

Formal check — inspect the svy: regress table:

  • Find the coefficient for “Female”.
  • Does the 95% CI for this coefficient include zero?
  • Does the formal output agree with your visual judgment?

Note: The coefficient in svy: regress is Female − Male. A negative value means women are less likely than men to have voted Reform.

Compare Stage 1 and Stage 2:

  • Do the unweighted and weighted results lead to the same conclusion?
  • If the estimates differ, which direction does the weighting move them?

Test 2 — Newspaper readership and ideology

  • Research question: Are Telegraph readers more right-leaning than Daily Mail readers?
  • Alternative Hypothesis (H1): Telegraph readers are more right-leaning than Mail readers.
  • Null Hypothesis (H0): No difference in mean ideology between Mail and Telegraph readers.

Stage 1: Unweighted

Execute: Run the descriptive summary and the ttest command.

Interpret:

  • What is the mean ideology score for Mail readers? For Telegraph readers?
  • What is the estimated difference (diff)? Which group is further right?
  • Does the 95% confidence interval for diff include zero?
  • Do we reject or fail to reject H0?

Stage 2: Survey-weighted

Execute: Run the svyset declaration, svy: mean, svy: regress, and the marginsplot commands.

Visual heuristic — inspect the plot:

  • Locate the point estimate for Daily Mail readers and the confidence interval for Telegraph readers.
  • Does the Mail point estimate lie inside or outside the Telegraph confidence interval?
  • Based on this visual check alone: would you reject or fail to reject H0?

Formal check — inspect the svy: regress table:

  • Find the coefficient for the Telegraph group.
  • Does the 95% CI for this coefficient include zero?
  • Does the formal output agree with your visual judgment?

Note: The coefficient in svy: regress is Telegraph − Mail. A positive value means Telegraph readers place themselves further right than Mail readers on average.

Compare Stage 1 and Stage 2:

  • Do the unweighted and weighted results lead to the same conclusion?
  • This example is chosen deliberately: the result does not change dramatically. What does this tell us about when weights matter most?

Summary

In this activity, you have:

  • Applied survey weights using svyset and svy: commands to produce design-corrected estimates.
  • Used a marginsplot to visually approximate whether two groups differ significantly.
  • Practised the rule of thumb: a point estimate lying outside the other group’s CI is a signal of a likely significant difference.
  • Verified visual judgments against the formal confidence interval for the difference.

The visual heuristic is a practical skill for reading published research. The formal test remains the definitive check.
