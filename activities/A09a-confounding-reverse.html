Seminar Activity: Confounding Variables and Reverse Causality

Statistics for Political Science — Week 9

Author

Leonardo Falabella

Introduction

The concept videos for this week introduced two reasons why a correlation between X and Y may not reflect a causal relationship from X to Y.

Confounding variables. A confounding variable Z affects both X and Y but is not caused by X. It creates a spurious correlation: X and Y move together not because one causes the other, but because both are driven by Z. In the videos, we used the positive correlation between healthcare spending per capita and CO₂ emissions as an example: national wealth drives both, so the correlation does not imply that healthcare spending causes emissions.

We use three criteria to identify a confounding variable. Z is a confounder if it:

  1. Affects the dependent variable (Y).
  2. Affects the independent variable (X).
  3. Is not a result of the independent variable (X).

Reverse causality. Reverse causality occurs when the assumed causal direction may be wrong. Even if X and Y are genuinely causally related, the data cannot tell us whether X causes Y, whether Y causes X, or whether causation runs in both directions. In the videos, we noted that higher CO₂ emissions may generate more pollution-related illness, increasing demand for healthcare — the opposite direction from what the original hypothesis assumed.

In this activity, you will apply both concepts to six new hypotheses. For each, work through the three questions with your group before moving on.

The Six Hypotheses

Hypothesis A

People with a library membership are more likely to get vaccinated.

A1. What is the independent variable (X) and the dependent variable (Y)?

A2. Identify one or more potential confounding variables. For each, explain how it satisfies the three criteria: (a) it affects vaccination uptake; (b) it affects library membership; (c) it is not caused by library membership.

A3. Is there a plausible reverse causal story? If so, describe it: explain how vaccination behaviour might cause library membership, or how the two might influence each other.

Hypothesis B

Famines are less likely in countries where there is press freedom.

B1. What is the independent variable (X) and the dependent variable (Y)?

B2. Identify one or more potential confounding variables. For each, explain how it satisfies the three criteria.

B3. Is there a plausible reverse causal story? If so, describe it.

Hypothesis C

Countries with fewer women in the national legislature tend to have more restrictive abortion policies.

C1. What is the independent variable (X) and the dependent variable (Y)?

C2. Identify one or more potential confounding variables. For each, explain how it satisfies the three criteria.

C3. Is there a plausible reverse causal story? If so, describe it.

Hypothesis D

Companies whose employees are more satisfied report higher profits.

D1. What is the independent variable (X) and the dependent variable (Y)?

D2. Identify one or more potential confounding variables. For each, explain how it satisfies the three criteria.

D3. Is there a plausible reverse causal story? If so, describe it.

Hypothesis E

Firms with higher levels of employee turnover have lower productivity.

E1. What is the independent variable (X) and the dependent variable (Y)?

E2. Identify one or more potential confounding variables. For each, explain how it satisfies the three criteria.

E3. Is there a plausible reverse causal story? If so, describe it.

Hypothesis F

Children who spend more time using electronic devices at a young age develop worse social skills.

F1. What is the independent variable (X) and the dependent variable (Y)?

F2. Identify one or more potential confounding variables. For each, explain how it satisfies the three criteria.

F3. Is there a plausible reverse causal story? If so, describe it.

Synthesis

Once your group has worked through all six hypotheses, discuss the following:

S1. For which hypothesis was reverse causality hardest to rule out? What made it difficult — was it that both causal directions were equally plausible, or that the data could not distinguish between them?

S2. For which hypothesis was a confounding variable easiest to identify? Was it the same hypothesis where reverse causality was most obvious, or a different one? What does this suggest about the relationship between the two threats to causal inference?

S3. In the exam, you may be asked to reason about a causal claim — identify threats to its validity and explain them clearly. Based on this activity, what are the key questions you should ask yourself when evaluating whether a correlation reflects a causal relationship?

Summary

In this activity, you have:

  • Applied the three-criteria framework to identify potential confounding variables for a range of research hypotheses.
  • Evaluated whether the assumed causal direction in each hypothesis is the only plausible one, or whether reverse causality is a credible alternative.
  • Distinguished between hypotheses where confounding is the primary concern, where reverse causality dominates, and where both are present simultaneously.

These are the core reasoning skills tested in the causal inference component of the exam.
